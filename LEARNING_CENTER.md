Educational information only. Nothing here is professional financial, legal, or
tax advice or a promise of profit. Options can lose 100% of premium. Short
options, spreads, margin, exercise, assignment, expiration, liquidity, slippage,
and pin risk can create losses or obligations larger than a trader expects.

## 1. Market and stock basics

Learn stocks, ETFs, indexes, sectors, exchanges, regular and extended sessions,
market capitalization, float, volume, dividends, splits, earnings, gaps, halts,
and corporate actions.

Understand bid, ask, midpoint, last price, order-book liquidity, and the
difference between market, limit, stop, stop-limit, and multi-leg limit orders.
A displayed price is not guaranteed to be executable.

Before selecting an option, explain the underlying trend, catalyst, liquidity,
key levels, expected holding period, event risk, and what would prove the idea
wrong.

## 2. Options contract basics

Know calls, puts, strike, expiration, DTE, premium, multiplier, option symbol,
moneyness, exercise style, and settlement type.

A call buyer receives a right to buy. A put buyer receives a right to sell. The
seller accepts the corresponding obligation if assigned. One standard U.S.
equity option usually represents 100 shares, so a $0.45 premium normally means
$45 per contract before fees.

Learn ITM, ATM, OTM, intrinsic value, extrinsic value, expiration breakeven,
opening versus closing transactions, and the difference between selling an
option and exercising it.

## 3. Reading an option chain

Read expiration, strike, bid, ask, midpoint, last-trade timestamp, volume, open
interest, implied volatility, and Greeks.

The last trade may be stale. A midpoint is only a reference. Narrower spreads,
useful volume, and open interest can help execution but do not guarantee it.
Contract selection should match expected move, expected holding time, maximum
risk, liquidity, event exposure, and desired Greek exposure.

Before submitting an order, confirm ticker, call or put, buy or sell, open or
close, quantity, expiration, every strike, net debit or credit, maximum loss,
and buying-power effect.

## 4. Pricing and the Greeks

Option premium combines intrinsic and extrinsic value. Pricing is affected by
underlying price, strike, time, implied volatility, interest rates, dividends,
and supply and demand.

- **Delta:** directional sensitivity to an underlying move.
- **Gamma:** how delta changes as the underlying moves.
- **Theta:** estimated effect of time passing.
- **Vega:** estimated sensitivity to implied-volatility changes.
- **Rho:** estimated sensitivity to interest rates.
- **Advanced Greeks:** charm, vanna, vomma, speed, and color.

Greeks change continuously and interact. Delta is not a guaranteed probability.
Near-expiration ATM options can have extreme gamma and rapid theta.

## 5. Volatility

Learn historical volatility, implied volatility, IV rank, IV percentile,
expected move, skew, smile, term structure, event volatility, and IV crush.

Implied volatility prices movement magnitude, not direction. A long option can
lose despite a correct directional call when the move is too small, too slow,
or overwhelmed by time decay and IV decline.

Compare volatility across strikes and expirations. High IV can stay high and low
IV can expand suddenly. Large premium often reflects genuine risk rather than
free income.

## 6. Charts, candles, and timeframes

Understand candle open, high, low, close, bodies, wicks, gaps, volume, trend,
range, breakout, failed breakout, pullback, consolidation, and market structure.

Higher timeframes provide context. Lower timeframes provide detail and noise.
Support and resistance are zones, not guaranteed single prices.

Mark prior highs and lows, gaps, VWAP, moving averages, trendlines, and volume
areas only when they change a decision. State thesis, entry area, invalidation,
target, and expected holding period before selecting the option.

## 7. Technical analysis

Study trend, momentum, volume, volatility, and level-based tools:

- SMA and EMA
- VWAP
- RSI
- MACD
- Stochastic oscillator
- ATR
- Bollinger Bands
- Keltner Channels
- Relative volume
- Prior highs and lows
- Pivots and gaps

Indicators describe conditions from past data. They do not independently
predict the future. Learn lag, false signals, divergence, confluence, parameter
sensitivity, and regime dependence. Several indicators built from the same price
series are not several independent confirmations.

## 8. Core strategies

Study long calls, long puts, covered calls, cash-secured puts, protective puts,
collars, synthetic stock, stock-replacement structures, straddles, and
strangles.

For every strategy know:

- Directional and volatility outlook
- Maximum profit
- Maximum loss
- Expiration breakeven
- Capital requirement
- Delta, gamma, theta, and vega exposure
- Assignment and exercise risk
- What happens if price does nothing
- Ideal and hostile market conditions

## 9. Spreads and multi-leg positions

Study bull call, bear put, bull put, and bear call verticals; calendars;
diagonals; iron condors; iron butterflies; butterflies; broken-wing
butterflies; ratio spreads; and backspreads.

Calculate net debit or credit, spread width, maximum profit, maximum loss,
breakevens, buying-power effect, and short-leg risk. Use one multi-leg limit
order when possible. Legging can create poor fills and temporary naked-option
risk.

Defined risk does not mean small risk, and a pleasant payoff diagram does not
remove assignment, expiration, or execution risk.

## 10. Risk management and position sizing

Define maximum risk per trade, maximum open risk, daily loss limit, weekly loss
limit, maximum drawdown, sector exposure, ticker concentration, event
concentration, and correlated exposure.

Position size from planned and maximum loss, not from unused buying power.
Distinguish premium paid, stop-based planned loss, true maximum loss, gap risk,
slippage, and assignment obligations.

Track portfolio delta, theta, vega, and concentration. A high win rate can still
lose money when average losses exceed average wins.

## 11. Trade planning and management

Write the plan before entry:

1. Thesis and evidence
2. Market regime
3. Exact contract or spread
4. Entry method and acceptable fill
5. Maximum loss and size
6. Target
7. Stop and invalidation
8. Time stop
9. Event and overnight policy
10. Adjustment or rolling rules
11. Exit method
12. Journal fields

Study partial exits, scaling, trailing stops, time stops, rolling, closing early,
and expiration decisions. Rolling closes one trade and opens another. It does
not erase the original result.

## 12. Expiration, exercise, and assignment

Learn American versus European exercise, physical versus cash settlement,
automatic exercise, exercise cutoffs, early assignment, ex-dividend risk, pin
risk, after-hours movement, broker liquidation, and expiration-week gamma.

Exercise or assignment can create or remove 100 shares per standard contract.
A spread can become unhedged when one leg is exercised, assigned, abandoned, or
handled differently from another. Never hold through expiration without knowing
the share and cash obligations of every leg.

## 13. Events and catalysts

Track earnings, guidance, investor days, dividends, splits, mergers, product
announcements, analyst actions, court decisions, regulatory events, Fed
meetings, inflation data, jobs data, and major expiration dates.

Confirm whether an event occurs before open, during market, or after close. An
expected move is not a direction forecast. Event trades can face gaps, IV crush,
halts, wide spreads, and poor stop execution.

Decide before entry whether the position intentionally holds through the event.

## 14. Psychology and journaling

Study FOMO, revenge trading, loss aversion, anchoring, recency bias,
confirmation bias, overconfidence, boredom trades, moving stops, and increasing
size after losses.

Use checklists, fixed limits, scheduled breaks, and cooldowns after rule
violations. Record setup, regime, contract, Greeks, IV, liquidity, entry, exit,
reason, screenshots, emotion, mistakes, and whether rules were followed.

Separate good-process losses from bad-process wins. A winning rule violation is
still a process failure.

## 15. Backtesting, statistics, and learning

Track win rate, average win, average loss, expectancy, profit factor, maximum
drawdown, consecutive losses, exposure, holding time, fees, slippage, MAE, MFE,
and results by ticker, regime, strategy, DTE, delta, and event context.

Avoid look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, data snooping, overfitting,
cherry-picked periods, and unrealistic fills. Use validation and out-of-sample
data. Paper fills may be better than live fills.

Champion and challenger models need fixed definitions, the same comparison
periods, realistic costs, a meaningful sample, and human review.

## 16. Accounts, taxes, and trading rules

Broker approval, cash and margin treatment, settlement, buying power, exercise
procedures, and active-trading restrictions vary by firm and can change.

Keep confirmations, statements, fees, assignments, exercises, expirations, and
cost-basis records. Tax treatment can vary by underlying, holding period,
exercise, assignment, straddles, wash-sale rules, and special contract status.

Verify current broker, FINRA, SEC, OCC, and IRS information. Tradysquids is not
brokerage, legal, accounting, or tax software.

## 17. Scams, myths, and red flags

Red flags include guaranteed returns, secret indicators, impossible win rates,
pressure to act immediately, unverifiable screenshots, deleted losses, signal
rooms with no audited history, impersonation, credential requests, remote access
to brokerage accounts, and payment requests involving crypto or gift cards.

Common myths:

- High win rate automatically means profitability.
- Cheap options are low risk.
- Delta is an exact probability.
- Selling premium is always safer.
- Defined risk means small risk.
- More indicators mean more confirmation.
- Rolling erases a loss.
- Paper success guarantees live success.

## Shared ticker management

Any Discord member may use `/ticker-add` and `/ticker-remove`.

- Additions must be verified as currently optionable through Tradier.
- The shared universe has a hard cap of **25 active tickers**.
- No more than **12 tickers** are processed in one rotating scan batch.
- Removing a ticker stops new scans but preserves trade history.
- Existing paper positions remain tracked until they close.
- Member changes are stored under local runtime state and survive deployments.

Sensitive controls such as filter changes, manual full scans, pauses, and resumes
remain owner-only.

Always confirm live quotes, contract specifications, current rules, and risk in
your own brokerage interface before making an independent decision.
